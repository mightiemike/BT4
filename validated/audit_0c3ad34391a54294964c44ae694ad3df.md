## Analysis

The external report describes a missing-slippage-protection ("zero `amountOutMin`") bug class in a Uniswap-style DEX wrapper, enabling sandwich/MEV attacks on `swapExactTokensForTokens` and `removeLiquidity`.

java-tron ships its own on-chain Bancor-style AMM ("TRX Exchange", `ExchangeCreateContract` / `ExchangeInjectContract` / `ExchangeWithdrawContract` / `ExchangeTransactionContract`). The swap-equivalent, `ExchangeTransactionActuator`, does implement the analog of `amountOutMin`: the contract carries an `expected` field and `doValidate()` rejects the transaction unless `tokenExpected > 0` and the computed output `anotherTokenQuant >= tokenExpected` [1](#0-0) .

However `ExchangeWithdrawActuator` — the analog of Uniswap's `removeLiquidity` — has **no minimum-output/slippage parameter at all**. `ExchangeWithdrawContract` only carries `owner_address`, `exchange_id`, `token_id`, `quant` [2](#0-1) , and the withdrawal amount of the paired token is derived purely from the current pool ratio at execution time with no caller-supplied floor to protect against price manipulation [3](#0-2) . The only precision check present is a fixed 0.0001 "not precise enough" rounding-error check against the pool's own just-computed ratio, not against any user-specified minimum [4](#0-3) .

This is the exact bug-class analog: a liquidity-provider account (an unprivileged, ordinary chain participant who created the exchange pool) can be sandwiched — an attacker submits an `ExchangeTransactionContract` swap in the same block just before the victim's `ExchangeWithdrawContract` to skew `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance`, causing the victim's proportional withdrawal to be computed against a manipulated ratio, then the attacker reverses the swap afterward to capture the difference. This directly mirrors the report's "missing `amountOutMin`/slippage protection" pattern, but on the `removeLiquidity`-equivalent function rather than the swap function (which is already protected).

### Title
Missing slippage/minimum-output protection in `ExchangeWithdrawActuator` enables sandwich attacks on liquidity withdrawal - (File: `actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeWithdrawActuator.java`)

### Summary
`ExchangeWithdrawContract`, the on-chain TRX Exchange's liquidity-removal transaction, has no field or validation equivalent to `amountOutMin`. Unlike `ExchangeTransactionContract` (the swap contract), which enforces a caller-supplied `expected` minimum and rejects execution if the computed output falls short, `ExchangeWithdrawContract` computes the paired-token payout strictly from the pool ratio at execution time with no floor the withdrawing account can enforce.

### Finding Description
`ExchangeTransactionActuator.doValidate()` requires `tokenExpected > 0` and re-validates `anotherTokenQuant >= tokenExpected` before allowing a swap to proceed [5](#0-4) , providing genuine slippage protection.

`ExchangeWithdrawActuator`, by contrast, only recomputes `anotherTokenQuant` proportionally from the exchange's current `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` and `tokenQuant` [6](#0-5) . Because the pool balances used for this ratio can be shifted by any preceding `ExchangeTransactionContract` swap included earlier in the same block (or in a block produced by an attacker-favorable ordering), the withdrawing account has no on-chain mechanism to bound the amount of paired token it will receive. There is no `expected`/minimum field in the protobuf message to reject an unfavorable outcome [2](#0-1) .

### Impact Explanation
An attacker can front-run a known/predictable `ExchangeWithdrawContract` broadcast with a large swap that skews the pool ratio in the attacker's favor, causing the victim's withdrawal to receive fewer paired tokens than the fair-value proportional share, then reverse the swap in a back-run trade to capture the extracted value. This is direct asset/accounting-value extraction from an unprivileged liquidity-provider account via ordinary broadcast transactions and Bancor-curve market math, matching the accepted "exchange/market math" and "asset or accounting corruption" categories.

### Likelihood Explanation
Exploitation only requires the ability to broadcast `ExchangeTransactionContract` transactions with favorable gas/ordering relative to a target's `ExchangeWithdrawContract` — no privileged role, leaked key, or malicious peer/node behavior is required. Any account holding TRX/TRC10 tokens participating in the exchange can act as both the victim (withdrawing liquidity) and, separately, any observer of pending or predictable withdrawals can act as attacker.

### Recommendation
Add an `expected`/minimum-output field to `ExchangeWithdrawContract`, analogous to `ExchangeTransactionContract.expected`, and enforce in `ExchangeWithdrawActuator.doValidate()`/`execute()` that the computed `anotherTokenQuant` (and/or `tokenQuant` returned) meets a caller-specified floor before allowing the withdrawal to complete, rejecting the transaction otherwise.

### Proof of Concept
1. Account A creates or holds a stake in a TRX Exchange pool (`exchange_id = X`) and prepares an `ExchangeWithdrawContract` to withdraw liquidity.
2. Attacker B observes A's pending withdrawal (via P2P mempool visibility or predictable behavior) and broadcasts an `ExchangeTransactionContract` swap that shifts `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` for exchange `X`, arranged to land in the block immediately before A's withdrawal.
3. A's `ExchangeWithdrawActuator.execute()` computes `anotherTokenQuant` proportionally against the now-skewed balances [7](#0-6) , yielding a payout to A worse than the pre-manipulation fair value, with no `expected`-style check to reject it.
4. B broadcasts a reversing `ExchangeTransactionContract` after A's withdrawal is included, restoring the pool ratio and realizing profit equal to the value extracted from A.
