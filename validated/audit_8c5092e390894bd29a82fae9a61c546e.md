## Title
Meta-transaction (NEP-366) relayers can be sybil-drained via crafted failing `DelegateAction`s that refund attached deposits to the untrusted sender instead of the paying relayer - (File: `runtime/runtime/src/actions.rs`)

### Summary
NEAR's meta-transaction feature (NEP-366) lets an untrusted, gas-less account (`sender_id`/"Alice") get a relayer to pay for and submit a `DelegateAction` on its behalf. The relayer's transaction purchases all gas and pre-funds all attached deposits for the inner actions. If the inner actions subsequently fail once forwarded to the receiver's shard, the protocol's balance-refund rule sends the deposit back to the `predecessor_id` of the failed receipt — which is the delegate action's `sender_id` (the untrusted party), not the relayer who actually paid for it. This mirrors the Connext "sponsor vault" sybil finding: an unprivileged party can repeatedly trigger a subsidized reimbursement flow that has no sybil/self-dealing protection, extracting value from the party funding the subsidy (the relayer/vault) with no loss to the attacker's own principal.

### Finding Description
When `apply_delegate_action` processes a `Delegate`/`DelegateV2` action, it builds a new action receipt with `predecessor_id: sender_id` (the delegate's inner sender) and forwards it to the receiver's shard, while the relayer has already burnt gas and paid the attached deposits upfront: [1](#0-0) 

The code comment makes the asymmetry explicit: *"If something goes wrong, deposit is refunded to the predecessor, this is sender_id/Sender in DelegateAction. Gas is refunded to the signer, this is Relayer... Therefore Relayer should verify DelegateAction before submitting it because it spends the attached deposit."* [2](#0-1) 

This is confirmed at the protocol-refund layer: deposit refunds always go to `receipt.balance_refund_receiver()` (the predecessor), independent of who actually supplied the tokens: [3](#0-2) 

The architecture docs describe the exact mechanic and acknowledge the risk without any protocol-level mitigation: *"what if the transaction fails execution on Bob's shard? At this point, the predecessor is Alice and therefore she receives the token balance refunded, not the relayer... there is a financial incentive for Alice to submit meta transactions that have high balances attached but will fail on Bob's shard."* [4](#0-3) 

Because `sender_id` in a `DelegateAction` is fully attacker-controlled and can be any account (including freshly created sub-accounts), a single attacker can repeat this pattern across arbitrarily many `sender_id`s — precisely the sybil pattern the Connext report describes for the sponsor vault: no mechanism exists to detect that the "different" sponsored parties are the same economic actor draining the same sponsor.

### Impact Explanation
Any relayer/gas-station service that accepts `DelegateAction`s from third parties and pre-funds attached deposits is exposed to unauthorized value transfer: the attacker crafts inner actions guaranteed to fail on the receiver's shard (e.g., a `FunctionCall` to a nonexistent method, or any action that deterministically errors after the receipt reaches the receiver), attaches a large deposit, and recovers that deposit to their own account while the relayer is left having paid the gas costs and briefly custodying (then losing) the deposit float. Repeating this via sybil accounts scales the drain with no cap, directly matching the "High" severity class of the referenced report (unauthorized value movement out of a subsidized/sponsor-style pool with no sybil protection).

### Likelihood Explanation
This is reachable by any ordinary meta-transaction sender interacting with any relayer that funds deposits on the sender's behalf — a common relayer/gas-station pattern explicitly supported and documented by the protocol. No special privileges, validator role, or network position are required; only a relayer willing to sponsor meta-transactions without additional off-chain trust/verification, which the protocol docs themselves flag as a necessary but externally-enforced precaution ("Relayer should verify DelegateAction before submitting it").

### Recommendation
Since this is an inherent design trade-off of NEP-366 (like the sponsor vault, "no total mitigation... possible"), the recommendation mirrors Connext's: relayers must not blindly sponsor deposits for meta-transactions from unverified/untrusted senders, and should simulate/pre-validate inner-action outcomes (or refuse large deposits) before submitting. If a protocol-level fix is desired, consider making the relayer (not the delegate's `sender_id`) the deposit-refund recipient when the relayer is the transaction signer, or exposing an opt-in flag for delegate actions where deposit refunds route to the paying signer rather than the predecessor.

### Proof of Concept
1. Relayer R agrees to relay `DelegateAction`s and cover attached deposits for third parties (a common "gas station"/sponsor pattern).
2. Attacker creates account `A` (or many sub-accounts `A1..An`) and signs a `DelegateAction` with `sender_id = A`, `receiver_id = Victim`, containing an inner action attaching a large deposit and calling a method guaranteed to fail (e.g., a nonexistent method name on `Victim`, per `apply_delegate_action`/`action_function_call` failure handling).
3. R wraps and submits it as a transaction, paying gas and reserving the deposit per `apply_delegate_action` (`runtime/runtime/src/actions.rs:499-519`).
4. On `Victim`'s shard the inner action fails; `refund_unspent_gas_and_deposits`/`new_balance_refund` sends the deposit back to `predecessor_id = A` (`runtime/runtime/src/lib.rs:1402-1407`), not to R.
5. Attacker repeats steps 2–4 across many `sender_id`s (sybils) to drain R's sponsored deposit float indefinitely, while R only ever recovers unused *gas*, never the deposit exposure it fronted.
