### Title
Missing slippage protection in `ExchangeInjectContract`/`ExchangeWithdrawContract` enables MEV sandwich attacks on TRC10 Bancor-style exchanges - (File: `actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeWithdrawActuator.java`, `actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeInjectActuator.java`)

### Summary
The AMO2 report describes an AMM liquidity-removal function (`rebalanceUp`) whose slippage bound is miscalculated, so it fails to protect against sandwich attacks that manipulate the pool ratio around a liquidity-changing transaction. java-tron's TRC10 Bancor-style exchange (`Exchange*Contract`) has an analogous liquidity-changing pair of operations — `ExchangeInjectContract` and `ExchangeWithdrawContract` — that compute a counter-token amount from the *current* pool ratio but, unlike `ExchangeTransactionContract` (which carries an `expected` minimum-received field [1](#0-0) ), provide **no slippage/minimum-received parameter at all** [2](#0-1) .

### Finding Description
`ExchangeTransactionActuator` (the swap path) explicitly checks the resulting counter-token amount against a caller-supplied `expected` value and reverts if it is not met, giving callers real slippage protection: [3](#0-2) 

By contrast, `ExchangeInjectActuator.execute` computes `anotherTokenQuant` purely from the pool's *current* `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` ratio at execution time and applies it unconditionally — there is no field in `ExchangeInjectContract` for a caller to bound the acceptable exchange rate: [4](#0-3) 

Likewise, `ExchangeWithdrawActuator.execute` derives the counter-token amount from the live ratio with no caller-provided bound: [5](#0-4) 

Both actuators are restricted to the exchange's `creatorAddress`, but that role is not a privileged/admin role in the java-tron protocol sense — it is simply whichever ordinary account called `ExchangeCreateContract` to set up the pool [6](#0-5) . Any unprivileged account can become a creator and is then exposed to this gap.

Because `ExchangeCapsule.transaction`/injection math derives amounts from mutable on-chain pool balances (`firstTokenBalance`, `secondTokenBalance`) that any account can move via `ExchangeTransactionContract` swaps just before/after the creator's inject/withdraw transaction, a block producer or bot observing the pending inject/withdraw transaction can sandwich it: swap to shift the ratio unfavorably immediately before it, let the inject/withdraw execute at the skewed ratio, then swap back afterward to restore the ratio and capture the difference — exactly the sandwich pattern described in the AMO2 report, except here there is no bound at all rather than merely a miscalculated one.

### Impact Explanation
This is a direct accounting/value-extraction impact: the exchange creator's `ExchangeInjectContract`/`ExchangeWithdrawContract` transaction can be forced to execute at an attacker-manipulated ratio, transferring value from the creator to the attacker with no on-chain mechanism (no `expected`/min-received field) to prevent it. This affects the TRC10 exchange/market feature that is reachable by any unprivileged account.

### Likelihood Explanation
Any account can call `ExchangeCreateContract` to become a pool creator, and then call `ExchangeInjectContract`/`ExchangeWithdrawContract` freely thereafter. Because `ExchangeTransactionContract` swaps against the same pool are also open to any account and can be ordered around the inject/withdraw transaction within the same block by a block-producing entity, the sandwich is straightforward to execute whenever a creator adjusts liquidity, making the likelihood non-trivial for active exchanges.

### Recommendation
Add an explicit slippage/bound parameter (e.g., `expected_another_token_quant` or min/max bounds) to `ExchangeInjectContract` and `ExchangeWithdrawContract`, and validate the computed `anotherTokenQuant` against it in `ExchangeInjectActuator`/`ExchangeWithdrawActuator`, mirroring the existing `expected` check already present in `ExchangeTransactionActuator`.

### Proof of Concept
1. Account A creates an exchange pool via `ExchangeCreateContract` (first_token/second_token balances set).
2. Account A submits an `ExchangeWithdrawContract` to withdraw liquidity, expecting to receive `anotherTokenQuant` computed at the current ratio [7](#0-6) .
3. A block producer/attacker observes this pending transaction and inserts an `ExchangeTransactionContract` swap before it to skew `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` in their favor, and another swap after it to restore the ratio.
4. Because `ExchangeWithdrawContract` has no `expected`/slippage field, account A's withdraw executes unconditionally at the skewed ratio, receiving a worse `anotherTokenQuant` than intended — value captured by the attacker through the sandwich, with no on-chain check able to reject it.
